Okay! Real quick, what's going on?
<a rel="nofollow"
  href="https://9to5mac.com/2026/05/17/spotify-confirms-disco-ball-app-icon-is-temporary-and-regular-logo-will-return/"
  title="9to5Mac: Spotify confirms disco ball app icon is temporary and regular logo will return"
  target="_blank">Spotify introduced a 20th-anniversary icon</a>
for iOS (and maybe Android, <abbr title="I don't know">idk</abbr>), and
<a rel="nofollow"
  href="https://www.reddit.com/r/Music/comments/1tgs4up/spotify_after_massive_user_backlash_over/"
  title="Reddit: Spotify after massive user backlash over logo"
  target="_blank">the Internet is appalled</a>.
At a glance, the icon looks like it's stuck in an update cycle, and up close,
it's the product of what I can only assume was prompted by some executive with
no design training and access to 
<a rel="nofollow"
  href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Firefly"
  title="Wikipedia: Adobe Firefly"
  target="_blank">Adobe Firefly</a>.
That same fictitious executive is also bragging about how they're redefining entire
industries in 
<a rel="nofollow"
  href="https://www.vlerick.com/en/insights/ai-feels-unprecedented-every-revolution-did/"
  title="Vlerick Business School: AI feels unprecedented; every revolution did"
  target="_blank">an unprecedented era that seems to have a lot of precedence</a>.

I digress, but I also have anecdotal evidence.

See… a trained designer, especially those in corporate, understands Design
Systems and Brand Guidelines, right?
<a rel="nofollow"
  href="https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/design"
  title="Spotify for Developers: Design Guidelines"
  target="_blank">Spotify has guidelines</a>
that say
<q cite="https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/design#logo-misuse">don't
use the logo to make new objects or shapes</q> (like a disco ball) or 
<q cite="https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/design#logo-misuse">don't
place the logo in a busy area</q> (…like a disco ball).

The new Spotify icon violates all those rules. Yes, it's true that "guidelines"
aren't law and can be overridden at any time, but I find it hard to believe that
a competent designer has willfully disregarded all five when simple adjustments
would have satisfied most of them. *That's* why it feels off. Both Spotify and
Apple spent time and resources developing their guidelines for cohesion. Every
other app on the screen followed the minimalist dress code, if you will, and
then Spotify shows up in an oversized sequin onesie. Like… <abbr title="oh my gosh">omg</abbr>,
we get it, it's your birthday. Chill out.

Because it's also strange to me that this gimmick was applied to mobile apps but
not to their web app or socials, which are *much* easier to change since they
don't require third-party approval. Although considering Mobile, Web, and
Marketing are different
<a rel="nofollow"
  href="https://www.lifeatspotify.com/find-your-team/job-categories" 
  title="Life at Spotify: Job Categories"
  target="_blank">job categories</a>,
that shouldn't come as a surprise.

Add to that the fact that, once loaded, the app itself doesn't use the disco
icon anywhere; this is what I suspect happened:
1. The Content Marketing team put together in-app promos for the 20th
   Anniversary.
2. The Mobile team decided that 
   <a rel="nofollow"
     href="https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/agile-dead-long-live-nicholas-gabrichidze-onjbe"
     title="LinkedIn: Scrum is dead"
     target="_blank">Scrum is dead</a>
   and everything will be Kanban (because who needs to plan work when A.I. can
   make changes so fast?).
    1. One of the head honchos decided it would be cool to make the app icon
       "pop" and threw a ticket in the backlog.
    2. The engineer is tired of A.I. and stopped caring a while ago. Prompted
       Claude. Closed the ticket. Told no one.
3. The changes went out, the Internet cried, the PR & Communications team got
   blindsided, and everyone in design and marketing remains frustrated because
   they exist for a reason… not to do stuff like this.
